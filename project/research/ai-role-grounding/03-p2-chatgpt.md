Then role-play 2 hiring-side people:
HR1: Branch manager at a vehicle-finance NBFC who hires field and collections staff
HR2: HR business partner at a private bank who hires for credit and analyst roles

For each JOB SEEKER, answer these questions in first person, the way that person would actually speak (casual, with Hinglish where natural, with specific stories, not polished):
1. Tell me about yourself and the job you're looking for.
2. Walk me through the last interview you attended, from the call until the result.
3. What was the hardest moment in that interview, and what did you say?
4. After the result, what did you find out about why it went that way?
5. How did you prepare for it, and have you paid for anything in your job search?
6. If you could change one thing about how you prepare for interviews, what would it be?

For each HIRING person, answer:
1. What roles do you hire for, and how many freshers do you interview?
2. Walk me through the last time you hired for one of these roles. How did you decide?
3. Think of a candidate you rejected recently. What made them fall short?
4. What happens with new hires in their first 2–3 months? Tell me about someone who left early.
5. How do you find candidates today, and what does it cost you in time or money?
6. If you could know one thing about every candidate before the interview, what would it be?

Rules:
- Be realistic, not optimistic. Include people who would NOT use or pay for an app like this, and say why.
- Include at least one person who uses ChatGPT or YouTube to prepare and thinks that's enough.
- No made-up statistics. Stories only.
- At the end, list: (a) the 5 strongest pains, (b) the 3 biggest reasons someone would NOT use HireMate, (c) 3 questions I should ask real interviewees that I'm probably not asking.
- Label the whole output clearly as "AI-SIMULATED — NOT REAL USER DATA".

Synthesis for HireMate
Again, these are research hypotheses generated from simulated interviews, not validated user research.
(a) Five strongest pains
1. Candidates don't know what “good” sounds like in a real interview.
P1 knows the answer but struggles to articulate it. P2 gives generic sales answers. P3 knows theory but struggles with practical judgement. P4 is technically capable but doesn't naturally think through business problems.
2. Existing preparation is mostly one-way.
YouTube gives answers. ChatGPT generates questions and model answers. Courses teach concepts.
The missing loop is:
Candidate answers → interviewer challenges → candidate adapts → feedback on the actual response.
That came through particularly strongly in P2, P3 and P4.
3. Candidates often prepare for the wrong thing.
P5 is the clearest example. His problem isn't “I need 50 interview questions.”
It's:
“What job am I realistically qualified for?”
That suggests a potentially important HireMate feature: role-fit diagnosis before mock interview preparation.
4. Candidates don't know why they failed.
Most participants received some version of:
“We are moving ahead with another candidate.”
There is almost no actionable feedback.
That creates a strong potential value proposition for HireMate: turn an interview attempt into specific evidence about what needs improvement.
5. Hiring managers care about behaviour that a CV doesn't reveal.
HR1 wants to know whether a person will actually go into the field.
HR2 wants to know whether a candidate will question inconsistent information instead of blindly approving a case.
That creates an interesting product gap between:
“Interview preparation”
and
“Can this person actually perform the role?”
HireMate has an opportunity to bridge those two.
(b) Three biggest reasons someone would NOT use HireMate
1. “ChatGPT + YouTube are free.”
P2 and P4 are important here.
If HireMate simply asks interview questions, generates model answers and gives generic feedback, there is a very weak reason to pay.
The product needs a defensible interaction advantage: dynamic role-play, interruption, follow-up questioning, role-specific scenarios and measurable feedback.
2. “I don't have money / I don't want another course.”
P1 is unlikely to pay as a fresher.
P3 has already paid for a course and doesn't want another one.
For this segment, a free tier or limited free interviews may be critical to acquisition.
3. “My problem isn't interview preparation.”
P5 exposes a deeper issue.
Someone may not know which BFSI job fits their current experience.
If HireMate says:
“Practise your Credit Analyst interview.”
but the person is unlikely to qualify for those jobs, the product is solving the wrong problem.
There may be two distinct user segments:
Interview-ready candidate: “Help me perform better.”
Career-transition candidate: “Tell me what I can realistically apply for, then help me get there.”
(c) Three questions I would add to your real interviews
These are more important than another question about “would you use an AI interview app?”
1. “Show me the last job you applied for. Where did you find it, what did you submit, and what happened after that?”
Don't ask them to remember generally.
Ask them to open Naukri/Apna/LinkedIn/WhatsApp and show you the actual journey, if they're comfortable.
You'll discover the real funnel:
Job discovery → application → recruiter call → interview → rejection → next application.
That is much more valuable than hypothetical answers.
2. “The last time you felt you performed badly in an interview, what did you do in the next 24 hours?”
This reveals actual behaviour.
Did they:

* watch YouTube?
* ask a friend?
* use ChatGPT?
* search interview questions?
* forget about it?
* apply for another job?
* buy something?
* practise aloud?

Past behaviour is much stronger evidence than “Would you use HireMate?”
3. “If HireMate disappeared tomorrow, how would you prepare for your next interview?”
This is the key competitive question.
Don't tell them about HireMate first.
Let them construct their current alternative:
YouTube + ChatGPT + friend + coaching + nothing.
Then ask:
“What is missing from that process?”
That answer will tell you whether HireMate actually has a problem worth solving.
The strongest hypothesis emerging from these simulations is not “Indian freshers need interview questions.”
They already have thousands of questions.
The more interesting problem is:
“I can prepare alone, but I cannot realistically practise being challenged by the person who is actually hiring me—and after rejection, I usually don't know what went wrong.”
That is a much sharper product-research hypothesis to validate with your real participants.
