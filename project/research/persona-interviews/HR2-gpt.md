# HR2: Ms. Rao, HR Business Partner

## Transcript

**Q1:** Tell me about the roles you hire for, and how many freshers or early-career people you interview.

**A1:** I handle recruitment for credit, underwriting, MIS and branch operations positions across our region, mainly graduates and people with up to three years of experience. We receive quite a few applications from MBA Finance candidates and engineering graduates who want to enter banking, but the quality is very mixed. Almost every resume mentions advanced Excel, Power BI or financial modelling, but when we give a simple business case, many candidates struggle to apply those skills. I wouldn't share exact interview volumes because that's internal recruitment information, but screening these profiles takes considerable time.

**Q2:** Walk me through the last time you hired for one of these roles. How did you decide?

**A2:** Recently we were hiring a junior credit officer, and the process involved resume screening, a written assessment and a case discussion with the credit manager. One candidate had only average marks in the written test, but when we gave her a self-employed borrower's case with inconsistent bank credits, she immediately asked whether we had business financials, existing loan obligations and supporting income documents. She didn't rush to approve or reject the file, and she explained what additional information she'd need before making a recommendation. The credit manager appreciated that approach, and after the usual reference and HR checks, we selected her.

**Q3:** Think of a candidate you rejected recently. What made them fall short?

**A3:** There was an MBA Finance candidate who answered almost every theoretical question correctly, including FOIR, DPD, CIBIL and loan-to-value ratio. But in the case discussion, we gave him a small-business loan application with irregular deposits and incomplete income details, and he immediately recommended approval because the customer had a reasonable credit score. When the manager asked how he had established repayment capacity, he kept repeating that the CIBIL score was good and couldn't explain the income assessment. We rejected him because, for a credit role, confidently making a decision without sufficient information is more concerning than admitting you don't know something; HR communicated that his practical assessment was below expectations, but we didn't share the detailed evaluation notes.

**Q4:** What happens with new hires in their first 2–3 months? Tell me about someone who left early.

**A4:** New hires generally go through induction, compliance training, system familiarisation and supervised work before receiving independent responsibilities. I remember one MIS analyst who joined from an IT training background and left after around six weeks because the work involved daily data reconciliation, checking discrepancies and coordinating with branches, whereas he had expected mainly Power BI dashboard development. He was technically capable, but I think we hadn't communicated the routine operational work clearly enough during recruitment. It's frustrating because replacing someone means repeating the selection and onboarding process, and the team has already invested time in training.

**Q5:** How do you find candidates today, and what does it cost you in time or money?

**A5:** We use campus placement drives, Naukri, LinkedIn, employee referrals and approved recruitment partners, depending on the role and urgency. There are portal subscription fees and recruitment-partner charges, but these are handled through our central HR and procurement arrangements, so I can't share the commercial figures. The bigger operational cost is that our hiring managers spend time reviewing profiles, conducting technical tests and interviewing people whose claimed skills don't match their actual capabilities. We've tried relying more on resume keywords, but honestly that doesn't solve the problem because candidates know which terms to include.

**Q6:** If you could know one thing about every candidate before the interview, what would it be?

**A6:** I'd want to know whether the candidate can exercise sound judgement when the information is incomplete, rather than simply recall technical definitions. In banking, if somebody doesn't know something and asks for clarification, we can train them, but if they make unsupported decisions confidently, that's a different concern. For example, an analyst should be able to explain why a report shows an unusual movement, and a credit officer should question inconsistent income information before recommending a loan. I'd value evidence of how they arrived at an answer, not just whether they selected the correct option.

**Q7:** If candidates came to you having already done a role-play mock with a score and a red-line check, would that matter to you? Would you pay for access to them, and how?

**A7:** I would be cautious, because a candidate's score from an external application doesn't tell me whether the assessment is valid, whether the candidate received assistance, or how the scoring was done. A red-line check might be interesting if it identifies something like approving a loan without verifying income, but I'd need to see the actual case, the candidate's reasoning and the assessment criteria, not just a green or red indicator. We also cannot start sharing applicant resumes, interview recordings or personal information with an outside platform without proper consent, information-security review and vendor approval. I wouldn't pay simply to access a database of supposedly screened candidates; if a vendor could demonstrate reliable, role-specific assessments using properly protected data, we might consider a limited pilot through procurement, but our managers would still make the hiring decisions.
